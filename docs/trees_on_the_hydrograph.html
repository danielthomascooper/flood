CAMELS-GB v2 · benchmark

Trees on the Hydrograph

A gradient-boosted tree was trained on 416 British catchments and tested on the twelve years after its training data ends. It is far more stable than expected and fails in exactly one place — and that place is floods.

The question was whether a non-neural model like HistGradientBoostingRegressor holds up on long-term stable prediction. Rather than answer from the literature, this is a run on the data in data/.

Stable over time? Yes, completely Median NSE 0.82 over a 12-year holdout, with no trend across those years.
Good at floods? No — structurally Median NSE −0.81 on each catchment's top 1% of flows; peaks under-predicted by 24%.
Free of feature work? No Remove the hand-built memory features and NSE falls from 0.82 to 0.35.
01

The setup

Model
One global HistGradientBoostingRegressor across all catchments — 400 iterations, 63 leaves, learning rate 0.08. Not per-catchment models.
Catchments
416 with a daily flow record at least 95% complete.
Split
Temporal, not random. Train 1970-10-01 to 2010-09-30 (6.0 M days); test 2010-10-01 to 2022-09-30 (1.8 M days). A random split would leak neighbouring days across the boundary and flatter the model badly.
Target
discharge_spec, mm/day.
Dynamic features
Rainfall at lags 1–7; rolling rainfall sums over 3, 7, 14, 30, 90, 180 and 365 days; rolling PET means over 7, 30, 90 days; temperature and its 30-day mean; day-of-year as sine and cosine. The rolling windows are a hand-built stand-in for catchment storage.
Static features
23 attributes — topography, climate, soil, hydrogeology, land cover.
Deliberately excluded
Every hydrologic signature — q_mean, runoff_ratio, baseflow_index, Q5, Q95. These are computed from the target; including them is leakage, and it is an easy mistake to make because they sit in the attribute tables looking like ordinary catchment descriptors.
Cost
254 seconds on 8 CPU cores. No GPU.
02

This is the part the received wisdom gets wrong.

Stability: it simply does not drift

Median NSE across the 416 catchments on the full 12-year holdout is 0.820. The interquartile range is 0.75 to 0.86; 244 catchments exceed 0.8, and only 11 fall below zero.

More to the point of the question: performance does not decay with distance from the training data. The twelfth year out is as good as the first.

This makes sense once you see why. The mapping from rainfall to river is a physical relationship that does not change; the catchments are the same catchments. There is no concept drift to suffer from. Tree ensembles are stable learners on a stationary relationship, and this relationship is stationary over decades.

03

You have to build the memory yourself

A catchment's response depends on how wet it already is. An LSTM learns that internal state on its own; a tree has no state at all, so it has to be handed one as features. To measure what that is worth, the identical model was retrained with the lag and rolling-window features removed.

Nineteen engineered features are worth 0.47 NSE — well over half the model's total skill. That is the honest cost of using a memoryless learner: the performance is real, but you paid for it in feature design, and the window lengths are choices you made rather than something the model discovered. Choose them badly for flashy catchments and you will not know.

04

This is not a tuning problem. It is what averaging inside leaves does.

The failure: floods

A median NSE of 0.82 is a statement about the whole record, and the whole record is mostly ordinary days. Restrict the same predictions to each catchment's top 1% of observed flows and the picture inverts.

−24%median bias on those days
297of 370 catchments score below zero there

Why it cannot be fixed by tuning

A tree's prediction is the mean of the training samples in a leaf. Averaging cannot produce a value larger than the largest thing averaged, so no prediction can exceed the largest target in the training set — and in practice it falls well short, because the extreme leaf still averages its contents.

The numbers here are stark. The training data contains days as large as 229 mm/day. The largest value this fitted model will ever output, across 1.8 million test predictions, is 93 mm/day. The test period itself contains a day of 244 mm/day.

On the 455 test days that genuinely exceeded their catchment's own training maximum, the model predicted a median of 0.68× the observed value. Being a global model helps a little — it can borrow high flows from other catchments, and 76 of 416 catchments do get predictions above their own training maximum — but the global ceiling is absolute.

05

How this compares

Lees et al. (2021) benchmarked LSTMs on CAMELS-GB v1 and reported a median NSE of 0.88. This run reached 0.82. Those numbers are not directly comparable — different catchment subset, different split, different forcing products, different features — so read the gap as suggestive, not as a measurement.

What the published comparisons do agree on is the shape of the difference rather than its size. A recent comparative study reports LSTM flood-peak under-estimation of 7–20% against 30–50% for tree-based models; the tree here under-predicts its top 1% by 24%, which sits in that range. The gap between model families is small in the middle of the distribution and large in the tail.

And the tail is hard for everyone

Martel et al. (2025) tested whether LSTM output supports flood frequency analysis and found results “mixed depending on catchments”, limited by “the inherent lack of rare events in the training dataset”. Neural networks extrapolate better than trees; they do not conjure rare events from a record that lacks them.

06

What to do with this

Use the tree when the target is the ordinary hydrograph. Water resources, baseflow, low flows, seasonal water balance, long simulation runs, attribution studies — 0.82 for four minutes of CPU and no GPU is an excellent trade, and the stability result means you can trust it years past its training window. Slater et al. (2024) used a quantile regression forest on British flooding for exactly this reason: interpretability and stability over raw peak accuracy.

Do not use it for flood magnitude. Not because it is inaccurate but because it is bounded, and the bound bites precisely at the design events flood work exists to estimate. A model that cannot output a number larger than 93 mm/day cannot warn you about a 244 mm/day day.

Update — the proposed fixes, tested

The paragraph that stood here recommended changing the target — log(Q), a catchment-normalised ratio, or high-quantile regression — as attacks on the ceiling. Running all three, plus a fine-leaf control, falsified most of it. log1p makes the tail worse (top-1% NSE −0.94 against the raw model's −0.81 — log-space MSE down-weights exactly the flood days), normalising by catchment rainfall moves it only marginally (−0.71), and finer leaves change nothing. Neither the ceiling nor leaf granularity was the binding constraint.

Only quantile regression delivers, and it does so by changing the question. The α = 0.99 model comes out genuinely calibrated (coverage 0.987 against 0.990 nominal) and covers the extremes: on the 455 test days that exceeded their catchment's training maximum, its median prediction is 1.21× the observed value, where the mean model managed 0.68×. But it is an upper envelope, not a point forecast. The diagnosis underneath: on the same flood days, this model family's own 99th percentile runs at 1.89× its mean prediction — the conditional distribution is that wide at the top, so any model reporting the conditional mean under-predicts extremes by construction. The failure was never the tree; it is the statistic.

Second update. “Calibrated” above deserves its own caveat, found later: the 0.987 is pooled over 1.8M test days, which ordinary days dominate. Scored only on annual-maximum days, the same α = 0.99 envelope covers 83% of events, not 99% — and adding real-time neighbouring-gauge observations lifts that only to ~90%, for tree and LSTM ladders alike. The full account, and what finally moved the flood numbers, is in the companion piece Where the Floods Went.

Found while running this

The first version of this experiment showed NSE collapsing from 0.80 to −0.21 across 2020–2022, which looked like textbook model drift. It was not. precipitation_cehgear, pet_chess, peti_chess and temperature_chess all stop on 2019-12-31 in the daily files and are NaN thereafter; precipitation_haduk, pet_hydrope, peti_hydrope and temperature_haduk run to 2022-09-30. The model was being fed missing rainfall, and gradient boosting handles NaN silently rather than raising.

Anyone building features across the full record must use the HadUK-Grid and Hydro-PE columns. The results above use those.

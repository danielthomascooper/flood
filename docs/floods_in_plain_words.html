CAMELS-GB flood-modelling project · reader's guide

Floods in Plain Words

What problem we worked on, how we went about it, and what every technical term means, with the definitions taken from the standard sources rather than paraphrased.

2026-08-30 · companion to Trees on the Hydrograph and Where the Floods Went

1. The problem

A river gauge measures how much water passes a point on a river each day. Given a record of past rainfall and past river flow, we want a computer model that predicts the flow on days it has not seen. That is a well-studied problem. Modern models do it well on ordinary days.

The specific problem we worked on is narrower: these models predict ordinary days well and floods badly. A model that explains 82% of the day-to-day variation in flow can still, on the highest-flow day of each year, predict a value about a fifth too low, year after year, in nearly every river. Floods are the days that matter for warnings, for insurance, and for deciding how high to build a bridge, so a model that is good on average and wrong on those days is not good enough.

We set out to answer three questions in order:

  1. Why do the models miss floods? Is it the type of model, the way it is trained, or something missing from the information it is given?
  2. What fixes it? We tested changes to the model, changes to the training target, a different model family, extra measurements, and finer-grained data.
  3. What is left after the fixes, and why?

2. Why this is interesting

Practically. Flood estimates feed directly into decisions about land use, infrastructure and emergency response. In the UK the standard methods (the Flood Estimation Handbook) rely on statistics of past peak flows; machine-learning models are increasingly used alongside them. If a model's peaks are systematically a fifth too low, a design based on it is under-built. Knowing why the peaks are low tells you whether the fix is a better model or better data, which are very different investments.

Scientifically. Three of our findings bear on questions the field is actively arguing about:

  • Whether the well-known advantage of one model family (neural networks with memory) over another (decision trees) on British rivers comes from memory of past conditions or from how the training error is weighted. We found it is mostly the weighting, which any model family can copy.
  • Whether a probability model that looks correct overall is also correct on flood days. It is not automatically, and we show why a "correct" one still under-covers the worst day of the year, for a reason that is a property of the question rather than a fault of the model.
  • Where the missing flood information is. Much of it is in the flows measured at neighbouring gauges at the same moment, and the rest is in rainfall measured hour by hour rather than day by day.

A fourth reason is about method. The project was run with controls, replications, and an adversarial review of its own claims, and two of its headline numbers were corrected as a result. That record is itself a useful example of how to do this kind of work.

3. The data

Everything uses CAMELS-GB, a public dataset for 671 river catchments in Great Britain (version 2, covering 1970–2022). For each catchment it gives daily rainfall, temperature, evaporation demand, and measured river flow, plus a table of fixed properties such as area, soil type and underlying rock. It also provides hourly flow and rainfall, and water-level records from 55 groundwater observation wells.

We used the 416 catchments whose daily flow record is at least 95% complete. Two rules applied throughout:

  • Time split. Models learn from 1970–2010 and are tested on 2010–2022, a later period they never see during training. This is the standard way to check a model has learned something that carries forward rather than memorised the past.
  • No cheating features. Nothing computed from the river flow itself (such as average flow, or how flashy the river is) is given to the model as an input, because that would leak the answer into the question.

4. The method, step by step

  1. Build the inputs. For every catchment and every day: today's rainfall, rainfall on each of the previous seven days, rainfall totals over the previous 3, 7, 14, 30, 90, 180 and 365 days, similar summaries of evaporation demand and temperature, the day of year, and the catchment's fixed properties. The rainfall totals over long windows stand in for how wet the ground and the underground stores are.
  2. Train a model. The model is shown the inputs and the measured flow for every day in 1970–2010 and adjusts itself to reduce the difference between its prediction and the measurement.
  3. Test it. The trained model predicts every day in 2010–2022. Those predictions are compared with the measurements using a fixed set of scores (section 5). The scores are calculated separately for ordinary days and for flood days, because the first experiment showed the overall score hides what happens on floods.
  4. Change one thing and repeat. Each experiment changes exactly one thing (the model family, the training target, an extra input) and re-runs steps 2–3 on the identical split, so that any change in the scores can be attributed to that one thing.
  5. Run a control. When an extra input appears to help, we also run a version where that input is deliberately scrambled (for example, giving each catchment some other catchment's neighbouring gauges). If the scrambled version helps just as much, the improvement was not coming from the information we thought.
  6. Replicate. Neural-network results depend on a random starting point. Headline results were re-run from three different starting points and reported as a range.
  7. Review adversarially. Two independent reviews were commissioned to find flaws in the methods and the conclusions. Their findings were checked and, where correct, the claims were changed.
In one sentence

Teach a model on forty years of weather and river data, test it on the following twelve, score ordinary days and flood days separately, change one thing at a time, scramble anything that seems to help to make sure it really does, repeat from different random starts, and invite attack.

5. The scores, defined

Every model reports the same set of numbers. Each is defined below with its source, followed by how it was used here.

Nash–Sutcliffe efficiency NSE
Definition

One minus the ratio of the sum of squared differences between predicted and observed flows to the sum of squared differences between the observed flows and their own average.

NSE = 1 − Σ(observed − predicted)² / Σ(observed − mean of observed)²

The value is 1 for a perfect model. A value of 0 means the model is no better than simply predicting the long-run average flow every day. Negative values mean the model is worse than that. Nash and Sutcliffe, 1970, Journal of Hydrology.

How we used it

Calculated per catchment over the 2010–2022 test period, then the median (middle value) across the 416 catchments is reported as "median NSE". Because the formula squares the differences, the largest flows dominate it, yet in practice a model can score 0.82 while under-predicting every flood: floods are rare, so their errors are a small share of the total.

Kling–Gupta efficiency KGE
Definition

A score that combines three separate comparisons between predicted and observed flows: how well they rise and fall together (correlation, r), the ratio of their spreads (standard deviations, α), and the ratio of their averages (β).

KGE = 1 − √[(r − 1)² + (α − 1)² + (β − 1)²]

A perfect model scores 1. Gupta, Kling, Yilmaz and Martinez, 2009, Journal of Hydrology.

How we used it

Reported alongside NSE as a check, because it separately penalises a model whose predictions have too little spread, which is exactly what a model that flattens floods does.

Annual maximum AMAX
Definition

The annual maximum series is the set of values formed by taking the single largest flow in each year of record. It is the basic input to flood frequency analysis, the statistical method used to estimate how large a flood of a given rarity is. Flood Estimation Handbook, Institute of Hydrology, 1999; the UK water year runs 1 October to 30 September (National River Flow Archive).

How we used it

For each catchment and each water year in the test period with at least 350 days of data, we take the day with the largest observed daily flow. That gives 4,950 "AMAX days". Two points of caution: the Flood Estimation Handbook uses the instantaneous peak within the day, whereas our records are daily averages, which are lower (on the catchments we checked, by a median of about 30%); and "AMAX bias" below is computed on the model's value for that same day.

AMAX bias
Definition

Bias, in forecast verification, is the systematic difference between predictions and observations; relative bias expresses it as a percentage of the observed value. Jolliffe and Stephenson, Forecast Verification, 2nd ed., 2012.

How we used it

For each AMAX day: (predicted − observed) / observed, as a percentage. We report the median across all 4,950 catchment-years. "−17.4%" means that on the typical worst day of the year, the model's value is 17.4% below what was measured. One limitation raised in review: the median hides that some catchment-years are over-predicted and some under-predicted; the plain average is close to zero.

Top-1% NSE
Definition

Not a standard score; it is NSE (as defined above) computed only on the days whose observed flow is in the highest 1% for that catchment.

How we used it

About 44 days per catchment. It asks: within the flood days, does the model tell the bigger floods from the smaller ones? A negative value means it does worse than predicting the average flood every time. It is a fragile number (few days, and any model that shrinks towards the average is punished), so we treat it as a diagnostic and never as the only headline.

Quantile
Definition

The p-quantile of a distribution is the value below which a proportion p of the values fall. The median is the 0.5-quantile; a percentile is a quantile expressed in hundredths. Standard statistical definition; see e.g. Koenker, Quantile Regression, 2005.

How we used it

Instead of asking a model for one number ("tomorrow's flow"), we can ask for a quantile: "the flow that tomorrow will stay below 99 times in 100" is the 0.99-quantile, written q99. A set of such values (q05, q25, q50, q75, q95, q99) is called a ladder in our notes.

Quantile regression
Definition

A method for estimating a chosen quantile of the outcome given the inputs, rather than the average of the outcome. It is fitted by minimising a particular asymmetric loss (the "pinball" or check loss) that penalises under-prediction and over-prediction unequally. Koenker and Bassett, 1978, Econometrica.

How we used it

Both model families were trained this way to produce ladders. Fitting each quantile separately allows them to cross (a q95 above a q99 on some day); we sort them into order after the fact, and for the neural network we built the ladder so that it cannot cross.

Calibration (of probabilistic predictions)
Definition

Calibration refers to the statistical consistency between the distributional forecasts and the observations. A q99 is calibrated if, over many cases, the observation falls below it 99% of the time. Gneiting, Balabdaoui and Raftery, 2007, Journal of the Royal Statistical Society B.

How we used it

"Coverage" is the observed proportion: the fraction of days on which the measurement was at or below the model's quantile. We compute it over all test days ("pooled"), per catchment, and separately on AMAX days. The last is the one that matters for floods, and it is not expected to reach 99% even for a perfectly calibrated model, because the worst day of the year is, by selection, more likely to be a day the river beat the odds. We simulated what a perfectly calibrated model of the same sharpness would score and use that as the benchmark.

Sharpness
Definition

The concentration of the predictive distribution: how narrow the predicted range is. Sharpness is a property of the forecasts only, not of the observations. Gneiting, Balabdaoui and Raftery, 2007.

How we used it

Measured as the typical width of the q05–q95 band in mm/day. A model can be made "calibrated" by predicting a huge range every day; the goal is the narrowest range that is still calibrated.

Probability of detection, false alarm ratio, critical success index POD · FAR · CSI
Definition

For yes/no events, count hits (event predicted and happened), misses (happened, not predicted) and false alarms (predicted, did not happen).

POD = hits / (hits + misses)
FAR = false alarms / (hits + false alarms)
CSI = hits / (hits + misses + false alarms)

Standard forecast-verification measures; Jolliffe and Stephenson, 2012; US National Weather Service verification glossary.

How we used it

The event is "flow exceeds bankfull" (below). This treats a flood as a yes/no warning decision, which is how it is used operationally.

6. The river terms, defined

Catchment
Definition

The area of land from which all surface water drains to a single point on a river (also called a drainage basin or watershed). National River Flow Archive glossary.

Gauging station; discharge
Definition

A gauging station is a site on a river where water level is measured continuously and converted to discharge. Discharge (or flow) is the volume of water passing a point per unit time, usually cubic metres per second. National River Flow Archive.

How we used it

CAMELS-GB expresses flow as specific discharge: the volume divided by the catchment area, giving a depth of water per day in millimetres (mm/day). This makes small and large rivers comparable; a flood of 50 mm/day is a large flood in any catchment. The hourly files use mm/hour, which is 24 times smaller for the same flow, a difference that caught us once.

Rating curve
Definition

The relationship, established by direct measurements, between the water level at a gauging station and the discharge. It is used to convert the continuously measured level into flow. National River Flow Archive; ISO 1100-2.

How we used it

Floods are usually larger than any flow ever directly measured at the station, so the curve is extrapolated and the "observed" flood flow carries uncertainty. The dataset gives this as a percentage band; at the 99th percentile of flow it is about ±11% at the median station. Any model bias smaller than that cannot be distinguished from measurement error.

Hydrograph
Definition

A graph of river discharge against time.

Bankfull discharge
Definition

The discharge at which the river just fills its channel to the top of its banks; above it, water spreads onto the floodplain. Leopold, Wolman and Miller, Fluvial Processes in Geomorphology, 1964.

How we used it

Provided for 318 of the stations in the dataset. Used as the threshold for the yes/no flood-warning scores.

Baseflow; groundwater-dominated catchment; chalk
Definition

Baseflow is the part of river flow that comes from groundwater and other slow stores, sustaining the river between rainfall events. A groundwater-dominated catchment is one where most of the flow is baseflow. Chalk is a porous limestone that forms Britain's largest aquifer; rivers on it respond slowly to rain and their flow is governed by the level of the water table, which integrates rainfall over months to years. British Geological Survey; CAMELS-GB attribute "fraction of catchment underlain by a high-productivity aquifer".

How we used it

"Chalk catchments" in our work means the 23 catchments where at least half the area is underlain by a high-productivity aquifer. This is a strict definition; several classic chalk rivers (the Ver, the Mimram) fall below it, which affected one of our conclusions.

Ungauged catchment
Definition

A catchment with no flow measurements of its own. Predicting flow there is the subject of a long-running research effort (the Prediction in Ungauged Basins decade, 2003–2012). Sivapalan et al., 2003, Hydrological Sciences Journal.

How we used it

Simulated by holding whole catchments out of training and predicting them with a model that has only seen other catchments. Every catchment is held out exactly once across five runs. Note that all our "ungauged" catchments are in a densely gauged country; the results do not automatically apply to regions with few gauges.

Donor catchment
Definition

In UK flood estimation, a gauged catchment whose data are used to improve an estimate at another, usually ungauged, site. Flood Estimation Handbook, 1999.

How we used it

We gave each catchment, as extra inputs, the measured flow today and yesterday at its three nearest gauges (median distance 13 km), each expressed as a fraction of that gauge's own typical high flow. A variant restricted donors to gauges on similar rock.

Nowcasting
Definition

In meteorology, a description of current conditions and a forecast for the next very short period (zero to a few hours), based mainly on current observations. World Meteorological Organization.

How we used it

Estimating today's flow from today's measurements at neighbouring gauges. This has no lead time: it tells you the size of a flood that is already happening, not that one is coming. When we delayed the neighbour measurements by one day, most of the benefit disappeared.

Forcing data; potential evapotranspiration PET
Definition

Forcing data are the weather inputs that drive a hydrological model: rainfall, temperature, and evaporation demand. Potential evapotranspiration is the amount of water that would evaporate from the ground and be transpired by plants if water were not limiting. Standard hydrological usage; PET after Penman, 1948.

How we used it

Daily rainfall and temperature from HadUK-Grid (the Met Office's gridded observations) and PET from the Hydro-PE dataset. Two other rainfall products in the files stop in 2019, so they could not be used for a test period ending in 2022.

Water year
Definition

A twelve-month period chosen so that it starts when stores are typically at their lowest; in the UK, 1 October to 30 September. National River Flow Archive.

7. The modelling terms, defined

Gradient-boosted decision trees
Definition

A model built from many simple decision trees added one after another, each new tree fitted to the errors left by the trees before it. Friedman, 2001, Annals of Statistics.

How we used it

Referred to as "the tree" throughout. It has no memory of its own; the rainfall totals over past windows are what tell it about past conditions.

Long short-term memory network LSTM
Definition

A type of artificial neural network designed for sequences, with an internal memory cell that can retain information across many steps. Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997, Neural Computation.

How we used it

Given the previous 365 days of weather as a sequence, one catchment at a time, with the catchment's fixed properties attached to every day. Its memory is reset for every prediction, so it cannot remember further back than 365 days; this mattered when we tested whether "memory" explained its results.

Training, testing and the split
Definition

Training is the process of adjusting a model to fit known examples; testing is measuring it on examples withheld from training. The split is the rule for which examples are withheld.

How we used it

Train on 1970–2010, test on 2010–2022, always. One weakness raised in review: a single split means every decision in the project was checked on the same twelve years, which can make late results look slightly better than they are.

Target normalisation
Definition

Rescaling the quantity being predicted (the target) before training, for example dividing each catchment's flows by that catchment's own average and spread.

How we used it

The neural network was trained on normalised flows, which means an error of a given size counts the same in a small river as in a large one. The tree was trained on raw flows, so its errors were dominated by the largest rivers. Much of the neural network's advantage turned out to come from this difference, not from memory.

Seed; replication
Definition

A seed is the number that sets the random starting point of a training run. Two runs with different seeds on identical data give slightly different models. Replication means repeating a result under such variation.

How we used it

Flood scores for the neural network varied between seeds by as much as the differences between models. Final results are given as the range over three seeds. One headline claim (that the best model beat the tree on flood peaks) did not survive this and was withdrawn.

Control experiment; shuffled control
Definition

A control is a comparison run identical to the experiment except for the one factor under test. A shuffled (permutation) control breaks the link between an input and the outcome by randomly reassigning the input, while keeping its values otherwise unchanged. Fisher, The Design of Experiments, 1935.

How we used it

Used twice. Groundwater well levels: the shuffled version helped exactly as much as the real one, so the wells carried no catchment-specific information. Neighbouring gauges: the shuffled version helped not at all, so the benefit was real.

Ensemble
Definition

A set of models whose predictions are combined, typically by averaging.

How we used it

The average of three seeds is reported as the most defensible single model. Taking the highest of several models' predictions each day gives higher floods but is not a calibrated estimate.

Oracle benchmark
Definition

A hypothetical model with some ideal property, used as a reference point rather than a usable model.

How we used it

We asked: if a model's quantiles were perfectly calibrated and exactly as sharp as ours, what share of AMAX days would fall under its q99? The answer, by simulation, was about 88%, not 99%. Our models scored 90%. So the models were not failing on flood days; they were at the limit of what their inputs allowed.

Temporal resolution: daily and hourly
Definition

The spacing of the data in time. Daily data give one value per day (here, the average over the day); hourly data give 24.

How we used it

Floods in small British rivers rise and fall within hours. A daily average smooths them, and an intense two-hour storm can produce an unremarkable daily rainfall total. About half the floods that every daily model missed happened on days whose rainfall total was not unusual. An hourly model recovered a large share of those, and a version without neighbour gauges recovered the same share, showing the recovery came from hourly rainfall.

8. What we found, in plain words

  1. The overall score hides floods. A model can score 0.82 and still under-predict every annual peak by a fifth.
  2. The under-prediction is partly built into the question. Asking a model for the single most likely flow gives an answer that sits below the actual flood on flood days, for any model, because the range of possible outcomes on such days is very wide and lopsided. Asking for a high quantile instead gives a usable upper bound.
  3. An upper bound that is correct over all days can still fall short on the worst day of the year, and most of that shortfall is unavoidable for any model with the same inputs. The right benchmark is a simulated perfect model, not 99%.
  4. The neural network's advantage over the tree on difficult catchments comes mostly from how its errors are weighted in training, not from memory. A small memory effect remains on chalk rivers. Our first, published, explanation was wrong and was corrected.
  5. Groundwater well levels, given as an extra input, did not help.
  6. The flows measured at the nearest gauges, today, help a great deal: the annual-peak shortfall roughly halves. This only works with no lead time, works in a densely gauged country, and does not remove the disadvantage of a site having no gauge of its own.
  7. What the daily models still miss is largely explained by rainfall that is intense for a few hours but ordinary over the day. Hourly rainfall recovers about two-thirds of those events.
What the numbers do not show

All results are for Great Britain, 2010–2022, daily averages, and one dataset. The "ungauged" results assume neighbours within about 15 km. The hourly results are from 60 catchments chosen because daily models did badly on them, and from two training runs. Flood measurements themselves are uncertain by roughly ±11% at the flows that matter.

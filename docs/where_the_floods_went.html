Where the Floods Went

A machine-learning model of British rivers can explain 82% of daily flow variance and still miss every flood by a fifth. This is the record of finding out why — through four model families, three controlled nulls, one public correction — of the two-line fix that was sitting in the gauge network all along, and of the adversarial review that cut two of our own headlines down to their honest size.

2026-08-28 → 08-30 · companion to Trees on the Hydrograph · every number below reproduces from experiments/ in the repo

§0The setup, and the rules of evidence

All experiments run on CAMELS-GB v2: the 416 catchments with ≥95% complete daily flow records, trained on water years 1970–2010 and tested on a strictly later 2010–2022 hold-out. Forcings are HadUK-Grid rain and temperature with Hydro-PE PET; features are lags and rolling windows of these (a hand-built stand-in for catchment storage) plus 23 static attributes. Nothing derived from discharge is ever a feature.

Every model reports the same card, built after the first experiment showed that headline skill scores hide everything flood work cares about: whole-record NSE and KGE, then separately the flood tail — NSE within each catchment's top 1% of flows, bias on annual maxima (AMAX, the number flood-frequency analysis is fitted to), and behaviour on days exceeding the training-period maximum.

Three rules of evidence held throughout, and each ended up mattering: every claimed mechanism gets a shuffled or ablated control; every headline number gets checked conditionally, not just in aggregate; and every interpretation survives, or does not survive, an adversarial audit against the recorded artifacts.

The failure is structural, not a tuning artifact. Halving leaf sizes changes nothing; removing the memory features (lags, rolling sums) collapses overall skill to +0.35, so the features carry real storage information — they just cannot carry the peak.

§2The conditional mean is the wrong statistic

Every mean-regression repair made the tail worse or left it alone. Training on log1p(y) — the standard advice — drops top-1% NSE from −0.81 to −0.94, because error in log space down-weights exactly the days that matter. Normalising by catchment rainfall is marginal. Only quantile regression at α = 0.99 covers the extremes, and it does so by answering a different question: an envelope, not a forecast.

The diagnosis underneath is the load-bearing fact of the whole project. On flood days, this model family's own 99th percentile runs at 1.89× its mean prediction. The conditional distribution of tomorrow's flow, given everything a daily model can know, is that wide at the top — so any model reporting its central statistic under-predicts extremes by construction. No target transform can fix this, because it is not a defect of the model; it is a property of the question.

The same experiment ran four times over the following days in different guises, and the pattern held every time: the median (q50) of a well-calibrated predictive distribution is a worse point forecast for peaks than the mean of the same feature set — by 13 points of AMAX bias in the last and best model. Distributions and point statistics must be chosen separately, on purpose.

§3Calibration can be true in aggregate and false on the day that matters

The full quantile ladder (α = 0.05…0.99) looked like a solved problem: every level within ~1.5 points of nominal coverage over 1.8 million test days, sharp central intervals, 90% of catchments holding q99 within two points. Then the same ladder was scored only on annual-maximum days:

A "calibrated" 99% envelope misses one annual maximum in six. Pooled calibration is dominated by ordinary days and says nothing about the days the envelope exists for; anyone deploying probabilistic flood bounds should score the events, not the record. One subtlety, found by adversarial review and then simulated: this is not a calibration defect, and 0.99 is not the right target. An annual maximum is selected on the realisation — it is preferentially an upper-tail draw of its own conditional distribution — so even a perfectly calibrated q99 of this ladder's sharpness would cover only ~0.88 of AMAX days (0.81–0.90 across tail assumptions). AMAX-day coverage measures the information content of the inputs, not the honesty of the quantiles. That reframing matters for everything below: a ladder sitting at its oracle ceiling has exhausted its feature set, and the only way up is more informative inputs.

Correction · published claim

The companion essay originally described the α=0.99 envelope as simply "calibrated" on the strength of the pooled number. That was true and insufficient; the conditional table above is the honest statement. The essay's §6 has been corrected once already (its original target-transform advice was falsified by §2's experiments); this is the second caveat the record needs.

§4The chalk story: a wrong mechanism, publicly corrected

A regional LSTM — one layer, per-basin normalised targets, the standard recipe — beat the tree overall (+0.855 vs +0.820) and, most strikingly, improved every one of the 23 chalk catchments, the groundwater-dominated rivers that published CAMELS-GB benchmarks report as the hardest class. We committed the obvious interpretation: hidden-state multi-year aquifer memory.

An adversarial audit of our own evidence broke that claim within a day, on four counts:

  • The trainer uses stateless 365-day windows — the same horizon as the tree's 365-day rolling rain sum. Multi-year memory was architecturally impossible all along.
  • The gain was not chalk-specific: the LSTM rescued all weak-tree catchments (those below 0.6 NSE gained +0.45 median, four times the chalk gain), and the improvement tracked low flow variance, not geology (flow-variance-matched chalk residual, p ≈ 0.11).
  • The five named "chalk failures" from the spatial experiment were not chalk by the project's own ≥50% definition (their high-productivity fractions run 5.8–33).
  • A named confound existed: per-basin target normalisation makes the LSTM's loss basin-NSE-like, while the tree's pooled MSE under-weights quiet catchments.

The discriminating experiments then apportioned the gain exactly. Giving the tree a scale-free target (log1p) recovered 74% of the LSTM's weak-catchment gain. Shrinking the LSTM's window to 90 days kept 87% of that gain — but halved the chalk-specific part. And extending the window to 730 days added nothing anywhere.

Verdict The LSTM's celebrated win is mostly loss normalisation — an objective-function choice any model family can copy — plus one real, small, properly-controlled residue: on chalk, and only on chalk, months 4–12 of forcing history are worth about +0.06 NSE. "Multi-year aquifer memory" is dead; "a season of memory matters on chalk" survived its controls.
The null that rhymes

Feeding each catchment its nearest observation well's level (z-scored on train-window statistics, 29 usable wells) changed nothing — and the control proved the nothing: a shuffled well assignment scored identically. Whatever regional wetness a well carries, a year of rainfall already encodes; single-well covariates cannot substitute for whatever storage signal is missing. Measured state is not automatically usable state.

§5The flood wave is already measured

Closing the two gates left one diagnosis standing: on the days that matter, the information is missing from daily rainfall — the conditional distribution is wide because the forcings genuinely do not determine the peak. But a flood is not an unobserved event. It is water, moving through a gauged network, telemetered in real time. The neighbouring gauges are the missing measurement.

Two features per neighbour — today's and yesterday's observed flow at the three nearest usable gauges (median 13.2 km away), each scaled by that donor's own high-flow level — and the picture changes more than everything else in the project combined:

The shuffled-donor control — every catchment given some other catchment's neighbours — moves nothing. The gain is the local flood wave being read off the network, not a regional wetness index. Event anatomy confirms it: with donors the model recovers 86% of the matched peak and 93% of five-day event volume, against 76% and 89% without.

Three honesty clauses, each earned by a control. Lead time: the value is strictly contemporaneous — donors lagged by one day retain only 14% of the gain, so this is nowcasting, never forecasting; by the time the neighbours show the flood, the flood is happening. Triviality floor: a zero-parameter predictor (nearest donor's flow, rescaled) already achieves +0.715 median NSE, so part of any donor model's skill is the spatial coherence of the flow field itself; the modelling contribution is the margin above that floor. Nestedness: the concern that the nearest donor might sit on the same river and simply measure the target's own water is real but minor — excluding it retains 88% of the gain, and the gain is flat in donor distance out to 43 km.

The ungauged version needs the most careful sentence in this document. A site with no gauge of its own still has neighbours, and with them an ungauged site matches what a gauged site scored without them (+0.784 → ~+0.83 territory). But the penalty is not closed: compared fairly — donors on both sides — the ungauged site still trails the gauged one by +0.048 median, +0.132 on chalk, because donors lift both settings. One refinement carried the hard cases: requiring donors to be hydrogeologically similar (mapped aquifer fraction — no gauge needed) before taking the nearest three cuts the chalk penalty roughly in half, at a cost of one kilometre of median donor distance. And for a few catchments that behave like nowhere near them, donors actively mislead; no weighting scheme we tried fixed the median and the pathologies at once.

The two ingredients are additive — the LSTM beats the donor tree on 76–80% of catchments in every seed — because they fix different things: normalisation redistributes capacity toward quiet catchments; donors inject the measurement daily rainfall lacks. The seed replication earned its place: the first seed's −6.6% AMAX bias and +0.201 top-1% NSE were the optimistic end of the distribution. What survives three seeds is a robust ordinary-day win, a top-1% NSE that is positive on every seed — genuine skill inside the tail, where the baseline scored −0.81 — and an AMAX bias of about −10% that ties the donor tree rather than beating it. At daily resolution the peak problem is substantially, not entirely, closed, and the last three points of bias belong to the donors, not to the architecture.

§7The residual, named

Substantially — not entirely. Put the same calibrated 99% envelope on both model classes, donors included, and score it on the 4,950 annual maxima: the tree ladder covers 89.6%, the LSTM ladder 89.2% (date-clustered 95% CI [0.883, 0.907] — one number, statistically). And both sit at the ~0.88 oracle ceiling from §3: the ladders have exhausted this daily feature set. That phrasing is deliberate — "the limit is in the data" would overclaim, since the two models share inputs, split, and resolution; what converged is the pair, and what the oracle shows is that nothing sharper was available to them at daily resolution.

The residual has names. Only 44% of the two ladders' misses are the same events; 225 annual maxima (4.5%) escape both envelopes, and they have a signature: summer floods are twice over-represented, and 55% of them occur on days whose rainfall is below its own 90th percentile — floods that daily rain totals cannot see, almost certainly short intense convective bursts averaged into invisibility. (Daily averaging also clips true instantaneous peaks by ~20% in the audited catchments, and 178 of 416 test maxima exceed the largest flow ever physically gauged at their station — the "truth" at the extremes is itself partly extrapolation.)

Whether hourly data recovers those events was the last experiment, run as a 60-catchment pilot (the 48 most-missed plus 12 controls, donors included at hourly resolution) with the missed events as its named, pre-registered success criterion.

Correction · the pilot's first headline

This section originally reported "roughly three-quarters covered, against 0% by construction." Adversarial review broke both halves, and its numbers verify. The 72–77% scored the hourly model against its own observation record, which on these event days runs at a median 85.5% of the daily-file values the daily models were judged against; rescored with one ruler, the hourly envelope covers 51.7% of the missed events on the exact day and 64.1% within ±1 day. And "0%" was rigged by selection: the events were defined as missed by those two ladders, but a third model recovers some through diversity alone — the weaker no-donor daily ladders jointly cover 24.8% of the same events.

The corrected verdict still stands, at honest size: 64% recovered against a 25% diversity baseline — a 2.6× recovery, and demonstrably information rather than envelope width (a daily q99 inflated to the hourly model's overall coverage captures just 4.1%). Ordinary-day skill holds at parity with the daily models. A material share of the residual was sitting below the daily sampling rate, and the deconfound says which part of the hourly data carries it: a rain-only hourly model recovers the same share of the missed events as the donor-equipped one (75% vs 77% on the pilot's own comparator; a second seed lands at 75%), while donors buy ordinary-day point skill (+0.88 vs +0.77). The event recovery is hourly rainfall — exactly what the anatomy predicted for floods born of sub-daily bursts. Two caveats remain: the catchments were outcome-selected, and the pilot is two seeds, not a study.

§7½From explaining floods to predicting them

Everything above estimates today's flow from today's information. The last experiment moved the target one and three days into the future, added the one input a forecaster uses first — the river's own recent flow — and judged every model against persistence (tomorrow's flow equals today's).

Three things carry over and one does not. The normalised LSTM keeps its edge over the tree; autoregression on the river's own flow is the main forecast ingredient; a calibrated one-day-ahead quantile ladder is achievable and covers the observed annual peak on 79% of peak days. What does not carry over is the neighbours: donor gauges add nothing at any lead of a day or more, in either model family — the nowcasting result was, exactly as its controls said, a same-day result.

And the forecast ceiling names the next constraint. Every model without a rainfall forecast is 34–40% low on the peak day; handing the tree the observed next-day rain — a perfect forecast — restores simulation-level skill and cuts the peak-day shortfall to 28%. At three days nothing sees a flood at all, and tree and LSTM tie exactly. The binding constraint on flood forecasting here is the rainfall forecast, not the model. The next experiment is not a better architecture; it is numerical weather prediction as an input.

§9What we would tell the next project

  • Score the tail separately, always. One number hid a 20-year peak deficit behind +0.82.
  • Point statistics and distributions are separate choices. The conditional mean under-predicts extremes by construction; the calibrated median is even worse. Four replications.
  • Condition your calibration on the event, not the record. Pooled coverage of 0.983 coexisted with missing one flood in six.
  • Control every mechanism claim. A shuffled control killed one appealing story (wells) and proved another (donors); an ablation killed a third (aquifer memory). The corrected record is in this document and the repo's history — including the claim we got wrong in public.
  • The cheapest information is often already measured. Six donor-gauge numbers per day beat every architectural intervention we tried, combined — though strictly with zero lead time.
  • Score both sides with the same ruler, and give every "0%" a diversity baseline. Our hourly headline shrank from 77-vs-0 to 64-vs-25 the moment the comparators were made consistent. Buy the adversarial review before the referee sends it for free.
  • Know your observation floor. Rating-curve uncertainty (±10.6% at q99), extrapolated peak observations (178/416), and daily clipping (~20%) bound what "fixing the bias" can even mean.
